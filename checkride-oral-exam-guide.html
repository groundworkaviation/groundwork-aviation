The Private Pilot Checkride Oral Exam: What DPEs Actually Ask

The oral isn't a trivia contest. It's a structured conversation that follows a predictable flight path, and once you know the route, you can fly it with confidence.

GROUNDWORK AVIATION · UPDATED JUN 2026 · 9 MIN READ

Every year, thousands of student pilots walk into a small briefing room, sit across from a Designated Pilot Examiner (DPE), and begin the conversation that decides whether they leave as a private pilot. Most of them are more nervous about this conversation, the checkride oral exam, than about the flight itself.

Here's the good news: the oral is the most predictable part of your entire checkride. Examiners don't invent questions out of thin air. Every topic they're permitted to test is published in the FAA's Airman Certification Standards (ACS), and nearly every oral follows the same logical sequence. This guide walks you through that sequence, the questions that show up in the first ten minutes of almost every oral, and the single answering technique that separates applicants who pass comfortably from applicants who talk themselves into trouble.

01What the oral exam actually is

The FAA practical test, the checkride, has two parts: a ground portion (the oral) and a flight portion. The oral typically runs 1.5 to 3 hours and covers the knowledge areas in the Private Pilot ACS: pilot qualifications, airworthiness, weather, cross-country planning, performance, airspace, systems, and aeronautical decision-making.

Two things surprise most applicants. First, the oral is not closed-book. You're expected to know core items cold, but reaching for the FAR/AIM or the POH to verify a detail is acceptable, and often viewed favorably. Second, the examiner is not trying to fail you. DPEs evaluate against a published standard, and their question style is usually scenario-based: "It's the morning of our flight, the oil temperature gauge is inoperative, can we go?"

02The first ten minutes are predictable

Nearly every private pilot oral opens with the same sequence, because the examiner is legally required to establish two things before anything else: that you are eligible for the test, and that the aircraft is eligible to fly it.

"Are you eligible for this checkride?"

Expect to walk through your logbook and show the aeronautical experience requirements of 14 CFR 61.109 and the eligibility requirements of 14 CFR 61.103: total time, dual instruction, solo cross-country time, night requirements, and your instructor endorsements. Flag these pages in your logbook the night before. Fumbling through three years of entries while the examiner waits is a poor opening scene.

"What documents must you carry as a pilot?"

Your pilot certificate (student certificate, for now), government-issued photo ID, and your medical certificate 14 CFR 61.3. Know your medical class and when it expires for your age and operation.

"Is this aircraft airworthy? Prove it."

This is the heart of the opening sequence, and it has three layers:

  • Documents on board. Part 91 requires two certificates in the aircraft: the Airworthiness certificate, which must also be displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance so it is legible to passengers or crew, and the Registration certificate 14 CFR 91.203. The Operating limitations come from a different section: the approved flight manual together with the markings and placards 14 CFR 91.9. Weight and balance is not a separate required document, it arrives as part of those operating limitations. And if you were taught a second R for the radio station license, that is FCC rather than FAA, and only bites on international flights or communications 47 CFR 87.18.
  • Required inspections. Annual inspection, 100-hour (when applicable, know when it applies), transponder and pitot-static checks, ELT inspection and battery 14 CFR 91.409.
  • Required equipment. The day/night VFR equipment lists of 14 CFR 91.205, what to do about inoperative equipment, and how Airworthiness Directives were checked and complied with.

"Can we legally fly today's flight?"

VFR weather minimums for every class of airspace your cross-country crosses 14 CFR 91.155, and we keep a full breakdown in our VFR weather minimums guide, plus day and night VFR fuel reserves 14 CFR 91.151 and the preflight information required by 14 CFR 91.103.

If you can move through that opening sequence cleanly, something important happens to the psychology of the room: the examiner relaxes. Examiners calibrate quickly, and a confident first ten minutes buys you a smoother remaining two hours.

03The middle of the oral: your cross-country, interrogated

You'll be assigned a cross-country to plan before the checkride, and the middle portion of the oral lives inside that plan. The examiner will pull on threads: Why this altitude? Why this checkpoint? What's your true airspeed based on, and what happens to your fuel burn if the winds aloft forecast is wrong? Where does your route pass near airspace that requires a clearance, an endorsement, or equipment you don't have?

Expect performance questions tied to the actual numbers, takeoff distance at this weight and density altitude, whether the runway at your destination works on a hot day, and a weather discussion built on the real briefing for the day. The strongest applicants treat the nav log as a story they can defend line by line, not a homework artifact they produced for the examiner.

Scenario questions dominate this phase, because the ACS requires examiners to test aeronautical decision-making, not just facts. "Your passenger needs to be home by 6 PM and the ceiling is dropping" is not a weather question. It's a judgment question wearing a weather costume. Answer it like a pilot in command: identify the pressure, state your personal minimums, and make a conservative call out loud.

04The three words that save orals: "I'd look it up"

THE TECHNIQUE

When you hit the edge of your knowledge, and you will, because examiners are allowed to find it, do not guess. Say you'd look it up, then actually find it: FAR/AIM, POH, or ACS. Knowing where answers live is an airman skill, and examiners score it as one.

Guessing is the most common self-inflicted wound in checkride orals. A guess turns one weak answer into three, because the examiner will follow your guess with questions you're even less prepared for. The applicants who struggle aren't usually the ones who knew the least. They're the ones who kept talking the longest.

The discipline is simple to describe and hard to do under pressure: answer the question that was asked, cite your source if you can, and then stop talking. Silence after a complete answer is not awkward. It's professional.

05How to prepare in the final two weeks

  1. Study from the ACS itself. It is literally the examiner's script. Every task lists the knowledge elements you can be asked about, and nothing outside it is fair game.
  2. Do a mock oral out loud. With your CFI, a study partner, or alone with a recorder. Knowledge you can't say out loud is knowledge you don't have yet. Teaching the material to someone else is the fastest way to find your gaps.
  3. Build a checkride binder. Tab your logbook endorsements, the aircraft maintenance records, your nav log, and your weather brief. Examiners notice organization because organization predicts cockpit discipline.
  4. Script your weak areas. For every topic that makes you nervous, write a three-sentence answer with the regulation reference, and rehearse it until it's boring.

06Frequently asked questions

How long does the private pilot oral exam take?

Most run between 1.5 and 3 hours. Well-prepared applicants who answer directly, and stop talking, usually finish faster.

What happens if I get a question wrong?

One imperfect answer rarely ends an oral. Examiners evaluate overall competence against the ACS. Acknowledging a gap and finding the answer in a reference demonstrates exactly the judgment they're scoring.

Can I use my notes or the FAR/AIM during the oral?

Yes. Core items you should know cold, but verifying details in a reference is acceptable and often a point in your favor.

What's the most common reason applicants struggle in the oral?

Talking too much. Guessing invites follow-up questions, and rambling answers expose gaps the examiner wasn't even looking for. Answer, cite, stop.

The oral exam rewards exactly the qualities that make good pilots: preparation, organization, honesty about what you don't know, and the discipline to use your resources. Fly the briefing room the way you'd fly the airplane, and you'll do fine.
