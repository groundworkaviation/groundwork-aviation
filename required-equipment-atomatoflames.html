ATOMATOFLAMES: Required Equipment, Decoded

The mnemonic every student chants and half misapply. Here's what each letter actually requires, the night-flying additions, and the inoperative-equipment question your examiner is really asking.

GROUNDWORK AVIATION · UPDATED JUN 2026 · 7 MIN READ

Somewhere in your oral exam, the examiner will point at something in the cockpit, a fuel gauge, a beacon, the panel clock, and ask: "Can we fly without that today?" The answer lives in 14 CFR 91.205, and the industry's favourite way to memorize it is a tomato-based tongue-twister.

01Day VFR: ATOMATOFLAMES

For powered civil aircraft with standard airworthiness certificates, day VFR requires:

  • Airspeed indicator
  • Tachometer, one per engine
  • Oil pressure gauge, for each engine using a pressure system
  • Manifold pressure gauge, for each altitude (turbocharged/supercharged) engine
  • Altimeter
  • Temperature gauge, for each liquid-cooled engine
  • Oil temperature gauge, for each air-cooled engine
  • Fuel quantity gauge, for each tank
  • Landing gear position indicator, if the gear retracts
  • Anti-collision light system, for aircraft certificated after March 11, 1996
  • Magnetic direction indicator (compass)
  • ELT, as required by 14 CFR 91.207
  • Safety belts, and shoulder harnesses for each front seat in newer aircraft

Notice how many letters carry a qualifier: if retractable, if liquid-cooled, for each altitude engine. That's where examiners probe. In a Cessna 172, reciting "manifold pressure gauge" without adding "but ours isn't an altitude engine, so it doesn't apply" tells the examiner you memorized the tomato and skipped the regulation.

02Night VFR: add FLAPS

  • Fuses, a spare set, or three of each kind, accessible in flight (if the aircraft uses fuses)
  • Landing light, only if the aircraft is operated for hire; the most-missed qualifier in the whole list
  • Anti-collision light system, for all aircraft at night
  • Position lights, on from sunset to sunrise
  • Source of electrical power, an adequate one, generally your alternator

03The question behind the question: 91.213

Here's the part that separates a pass from a struggle. When the examiner asks about a dead clock or an inop autopilot, they're not testing the mnemonic. They're testing whether you know the inoperative equipment decision path of 14 CFR 91.213. Without a minimum equipment list, the flow is: is the item required by 91.205? By the aircraft's equipment list or kinds-of-operations list? By an AD? If no to all, the item must be deactivated or removed, and placarded "INOPERATIVE", and then you, as pilot in command, still make the final call that the aircraft is safe for this flight.

Walk that flow out loud in the oral, rule, source, decision, and you've demonstrated exactly the airmanship the oral exam is built to find.

?Frequently asked questions

Do I need a landing light at night?

Only if the aircraft is operated for hire. For a private flight it's smart, but it isn't required by 91.205.

Does my trainer need a manifold pressure gauge?

Only altitude engines, turbocharged or supercharged, require one. A normally aspirated trainer doesn't.

What if something breaks that isn't on the list?

Run the 91.213 flow: not required by 91.205, the equipment list, or an AD → deactivate or remove it, placard it INOPERATIVE, and make a PIC judgment that the flight is safe.

Learn the list, but more importantly learn its qualifiers and the 91.213 flow behind it. The mnemonic gets you to the answer; the regulation is the answer.
