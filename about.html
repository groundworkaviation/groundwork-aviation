The pilot behind Groundwork

I fly for a living. I am not an FAA examiner and I will never pretend to be one. Here is exactly what I am, what I am not, and why I think the difference works in your favour.

Chris
Founder, Groundwork Aviation
Australian airline pilot, roughly 8,000 hours

Most aviation study material is written by people who teach flying. This is written by someone who does the flying, day after day, in an airline operation where a bad decision is not a discussion topic. That is the entire premise of Groundwork, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not mean.

The short version

I am an Australian airline First Officer with about 8,000 hours. I was a flight instructor in Australia, and I have flown business jets in the United States. I have never held a US CFI certificate and I have never been a Designated Pilot Examiner. I do not teach you what your examiner will ask. I teach you how a professional crew decides.

The honest ledger

The fastest way to lose your trust would be to blur this line, so I will draw it myself, plainly, before anyone else does it for me.

What I am

  • An airline pilot currently flying the line, roughly 8,000 hours
  • A former flight instructor, in Australia
  • Experienced on US business jets
  • Trained, checked and re-checked in crew resource management, threat and error management, and standard operating procedures
  • Someone who makes real go/no-go calls, with real consequences, on a schedule that does not care how I feel that morning

What I am not

  • A US Certificated Flight Instructor. I have never held one
  • A Designated Pilot Examiner. I have never been one
  • Able to tell you what your DPE will ask you, and anyone who promises that is guessing
  • A substitute for your CFI, who knows you, your aircraft and your local conditions
  • A regulator. Verify everything against the current FAR/AIM and ACS

If that second column disqualifies me in your eyes, I would rather you know it on this page than discover it later. But I would ask you to consider what the first column is actually worth, because I think the industry has undervalued it for a long time.

Why an airline pilot is writing for student pilots

Here is the statistic that started this: roughly eight out of ten student pilots never finish. They do not wash out on a checkride. Most simply fade. The logbook goes quiet, the lessons stop, and one day they realise they are not going back.

Ask why, and the answer is almost never a lack of talent. It is confidence collapsing under a decision they never felt equipped to make. Should I go today? Is that cloud a problem? Am I being sensible or am I being soft? Nobody hands a student a framework for that, and so they carry it alone until the weight of it quietly ends their flying.

The thing airlines figured out first

Decades ago the airline industry worked out that its crashes were not being caused by pilots who could not fly. They were being caused by good pilots making poor decisions under pressure. The response was crew resource management: teaching judgment as an explicit, trainable skill rather than something you were supposed to absorb by osmosis. That training is the water I swim in.

Flight school teaches you to fly the aircraft. It is very good at that. What it rarely has time for is the part that decides whether you have a long, safe flying life or a short one: how to think, how to weigh a threat, how to say no when everything in you wants to say yes. That gap is where Groundwork lives.

What the guides actually are

I want to be as careful here as I was above. The playbooks are researched study guides. They are built from primary sources, the FAR/AIM, the ACS, and FAA advisory material, written in plain English rather than regulator prose, and I do an accuracy pass on every factual claim before anything goes on sale.

They are not insider knowledge from an examiner's desk. They are structure: the material organised the way a professional would organise it, with the judgment layer that most study material leaves out. If you want a bank of exam questions, plenty of excellent US companies sell you that, and some of them are very good. What they do not sell you is a way of thinking.

The accent question

Yes, I am Australian, and yes, I am writing for American student pilots. The regulations differ. Judgment does not. Weather does not negotiate based on which country issued your certificate, and neither does a hot, high, heavy runway on a summer afternoon. Where something is FAA-specific I say so and I cite it. Where it is universal airmanship, it is universal.
