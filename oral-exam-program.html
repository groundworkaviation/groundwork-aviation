The mechanic

Examiners do not ask questions. They ask chains.

One question is a warm-up. The examiner is listening to your answer to find the next question. They keep going until they reach the edge of what you actually understand, and that edge is the thing being tested.

Here is one chain from Module 2, adapted from the program. Answer it in your head before you reveal anything.

Module 2 · ACS I.B Airworthiness Requirements · Chain 02
LEVEL 1

What documents must be on board this aircraft?

Most students say ARROW and stop. The answer that holds up separates where each item actually comes from, because "under which regulation?" is the natural next question.

Airworthiness certificate and Registration certificate are the two certificates § 91.203 requires to be in the aircraft. The airworthiness certificate must also be displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance, legible to passengers or crew.

14 CFR 91.203(a)(1), (a)(2), 91.203(b)

Operating limitations come from a different section entirely.

14 CFR 91.9(b)

The second R is not an FAA requirement at all. The radio station license is an FCC matter, and for a domestic flight in a US aircraft not required to carry a radio, the station is licensed by rule and needs no individual license. You only need one for international flights or international communications.

47 CFR 87.18(b)

And the W is not a separate certificate. Weight and balance information arrives with the operating limitations rather than alongside them: for an aircraft with an approved flight manual it is in that manual, and otherwise it sits in the approved manual material. Which is exactly what the next question is about.

14 CFR 91.9(b), 21.5(b)(1)

Why they asked: everyone has the mnemonic. The examiner is listening for whether you know where each item comes from, because that is what the follow-up tests.
LEVEL 2

Show me the operating limitations on this airplane.

This is where most students reach for the POH and stop. The operating limitations are not one document. They are the approved Airplane Flight Manual together with the markings and the placards, and for aircraft that do not require an AFM, an approved flight manual, approved manual material, markings and placards, in any combination.

14 CFR 91.9(a) and 91.9(b)(2)

Why they asked: they want to know whether you learned a mnemonic or learned the regulation. Pointing at the POH alone answers the letter and misses the rule.
LEVEL 3

This placard by the fuel selector is faded and you cannot read it. Are you legal to fly?

No, and the reasoning matters more than the answer.

If that placard is required, it is part of the operating limitations you must comply with. If you cannot read it, you cannot comply with it.

14 CFR 91.9(a)

And the call is yours. No person may operate an aircraft unless it is in an airworthy condition, and the pilot in command is the one responsible for determining whether it is in condition for safe flight. Not the owner, and not whoever told you it was fine.

14 CFR 91.7(a) and 91.7(b)

Why they asked: this is the real question, and the first two existed to set it up. It tests whether you can get from "I cannot read it" to "therefore not airworthy" without being led there. Handle it, and the next one is who is allowed to replace it.

Every chain is built this way and carries its primary source, so you can check it yourself. Nothing is sourced from another course. These are teaching scenarios: an airworthiness call on a real airplane goes to your CFI and your mechanic, not to a study guide.

What is inside

The tasks the oral is built around

Area I, Preflight Preparation, is where most of the oral lives. These are its tasks for single- and multi-engine land airplanes, plus the two Area II tasks examiners routinely fold in. Seaplane applicants have one further task, Area I Task I, which this program does not cover. The examiner can also ask knowledge questions from any Area of Operation, and does so throughout the test.

Built on FAA-S-ACS-6C, the current Private Pilot ACS, which supersedes 6B. Worth checking which revision your other study material is built on.

The examiner asks about your airplane, not a diagram. So this module is built around yours.

The part most people skim, and the part with the shortest answers.

II

Preflight Procedures

Preflight assessment and flight deck management, which examiners fold into the oral.

And three things you will not get from a lecture course

  • The Aircraft Profile Workbook. You fill it in for the airplane you will actually fly: V-speeds, fuel and electrical systems, weight and balance envelope, equipment list, inspection status. The examiner is not asking about a generic trainer. They are asking about the one on the ramp.
  • Six full mock orals, start to finish, in the examiner's voice, with the reasoning behind each follow-up shown in the margin.
  • An ACS self-check that maps what you have covered against every code in the standard, so you can see your own gaps instead of guessing at them.

The self-check shows you what you have and have not covered. It does not tell you that you are ready for your checkride. That call belongs to your CFI. This program is not a substitute for your flight instructor, the regulations, or the ACS itself.
