VFR Weather Minimums, Explained Simply

Six airspace classes, two altitude bands, a day-versus-night split: the 91.155 table looks built to be memorized and forgotten. It isn't. There's one idea underneath it, and once you see it, the numbers almost arrange themselves.

Written & vetted by a working airline pilot · 8,000+ hours

Open the regulations to VFR weather minimums and you meet a wall of numbers. Most students cram it for the written test, then freeze when an examiner asks the same thing a different way: “you're at 9,500 feet in Class E, what visibility do you need?” The numbers were never the hard part. The structure was. This guide walks you through 14 CFR 91.155 in plain English: the minimums for each airspace class, why they sit where they do, and how to read the whole table at a glance.

The short version

In most controlled airspace below 10,000 ft, Classes C, D, and E, you need 3 statute miles visibility and to stay 500 ft below, 1,000 ft above, 2,000 ft horizontally from cloud (the “3-152” rule). Class B is the exception: 3 miles and simply clear of clouds. Above 10,000 ft MSL it jumps to 5 miles with bigger margins (“5-111”). Class G is the most relaxed, and the only one where the rules change at night.

Why the numbers exist (this is the whole trick)

VFR flight runs on a single principle: see and avoid. You are personally responsible for not hitting other aircraft, terrain, or cloud, using your own eyes. Every number in 91.155 exists to guarantee you can see far enough, and stay clear enough of cloud, to do that safely.

Two things push the minimums up: speed and traffic. The faster traffic can close on you, the further you need to see and the bigger a buffer you want around cloud. That's why the table changes at 10,000 feet MSL. Above it, aircraft are no longer held to the 250-knot speed limit, so they can arrive much faster. You're given 5 miles of visibility and a full 1,000 ft / 1,000 ft / 1 mile cloud buffer to compensate.

The clever exception is Class B, the busiest airspace there is, yet its cloud rule is the most relaxed: just “clear of clouds.” Why? Because inside Class B, ATC provides positive separation between every aircraft. The system is keeping you apart, not only your eyeballs, so you're allowed to operate closer to cloud. Read the table through this lens and it stops being arbitrary.

The three patterns to actually memorize

You don't need eleven rows in your head. You need three patterns. The rest are variations.

5-111

5 miles · 1,000 above · 1,000 below · 1 mile horizontal. Bigger, faster, more room.

Class G is just “the relaxed one”: down low in the day you only need 1 mile and to stay clear of clouds, but the moment it's night, Class G tightens up to look like the 3-152 controlled-airspace rule. Remember it as “Class G goes easy by day, plays by the grown-up rules at night.”

How to use this on a real flight

Knowing the table is step one. Applying it means looking at the actual weather for your route and comparing. That's where the reports come in: you pull the METARs and TAFs for your departure, route, and destination, read the visibility and cloud, and check them against the minimums for the airspace you'll fly through.

One more thing every good pilot does: fly to personal minimums, not just legal ones. The regulation is the floor, not the goal. A low-time pilot launching into 3 miles of haze at night is legal and unwise. Set your own limits above the regulation and stick to them.

Four mistakes that catch students

  • It's flight visibility, not the reported number. The rule is about what you can actually see from the cockpit in flight, which can differ from the ground visibility in a METAR. The report informs your decision; it isn't automatically the legal figure.
  • Class G at night is the trap. It's the only class where day and night differ. Students memorize the easy daytime “1 mile, clear of clouds” and forget it tightens after dark.
  • The 10,000-foot line is MSL, and only above 1,200 AGL. Don't mix up the altitude references. The 5-mile rule kicks in at 10,000 ft mean sea level.
  • “Clear of clouds” belongs to Class B (and low Class G by day) only. Don't apply it in C, D, or E. There you owe the full 500/1,000/2,000.
On the checkride

Examiners love this topic because it blends a memorized rule with real judgment. Expect to be asked not just what the minimums are, but why, and to apply them to the airspace on your planned route. Being able to explain the “see and avoid” logic, not just recite numbers, is exactly what separates a pass from a stumble. (Our forthcoming Checkride Oral Playbook drills precisely this.)

Frequently asked

What are the VFR minimums in Class B?

3 statute miles flight visibility and clear of clouds. Class B is the only controlled airspace where you don't need the 500/1,000/2,000 cloud clearance, because ATC separates all traffic inside it.

What does “3-152” mean?

It's the memory aid for the standard rule in Classes C, D, and E below 10,000 ft: 3 miles visibility, 1,000 ft above cloud, 500 ft below, 2,000 ft horizontal.

Are VFR weather minimums different at night?

Only in Class G (uncontrolled airspace). At night, the relaxed daytime Class G minimums tighten to match the controlled-airspace standard. Classes B, C, D, and E don't change with day or night.

What's the visibility minimum above 10,000 feet?

5 statute miles, with cloud clearances of 1,000 ft below, 1,000 ft above, and 1 statute mile horizontal, because faster traffic is allowed up there, so you need more room to see and avoid.
