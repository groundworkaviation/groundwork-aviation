Aerodynamics for the Checkride, Without the Physics Degree

Lift, load factor, stalls, and the left turning tendencies. The handful of ideas examiners say students misunderstand most, explained the way a working pilot actually thinks about them.

GROUNDWORK AVIATION · UPDATED JUN 2026 · 9 MIN READ

Aerodynamics intimidates students because it gets taught as physics. On a checkride it is not a physics exam. The examiner wants to know that you understand why the aircraft does what it does, well enough to stay ahead of it. That takes a few core ideas, not a textbook. Get these right and most of the others fall into place.

01The four forces, and the one that matters most

In steady flight, four forces act on your aircraft. Lift opposes weight. Thrust opposes drag. When they balance, the aircraft holds altitude and speed. That much every student knows. The useful insight is that these forces are constantly traded, not fixed. Pitch up and you trade airspeed for a higher angle of attack. Add power and you trade fuel for thrust that the aircraft turns into climb or speed. Flying well is mostly the management of those trades.

Lift itself comes from the wing deflecting air downward, and the amount depends on a short list of factors: the shape of the wing, the density of the air, the speed of the airflow, and above all the angle at which the wing meets that air. That last one, the angle of attack, is the variable you control most directly, and it is the key to understanding stalls.

02The stall, properly understood

Here is the single most important sentence in this article. A wing stalls when it exceeds its critical angle of attack, and it will do that at any airspeed, in any attitude, at any power setting.

Students learn a stall speed from the POH and conclude that the wing stalls at that speed. It does not. The published number assumes one specific situation: one G, maximum gross weight, wings level, no acceleration. Change any of those and the airspeed at which the wing reaches its critical angle changes too. Pull hard in a steep turn and the wing can stall well above the book number. Hang on the propeller in a nose high climb and it can stall there as well. The angle of attack is what stalls the wing. The airspeed is just a convenient proxy that is only accurate in the one condition the book assumed.

This is the distinction examiners probe, because it is the difference between a pilot who watches a number and a pilot who understands the wing. Recover by reducing the angle of attack, which means lowering the nose to reduce the angle between the wing and the relative wind. Adding power helps the recovery but the angle of attack is what ends the stall.

THE EXAMINER'S FAVOURITE

If you remember nothing else: a wing always stalls at the same critical angle of attack, never at a single fixed airspeed. Say that clearly in your oral and you have answered half the aerodynamics section before the follow up arrives.

03Load factor and the accelerated stall

Load factor is simply how many times its own weight the aircraft is effectively supporting, expressed in G. In straight and level flight it is one G. The moment you bank and turn, the wings have to support both the weight of the aircraft and the force that curves its path, so load factor climbs.

The numbers are worth knowing because they are not linear. A level turn at 30 degrees of bank is a gentle 1.15 G. At 60 degrees of bank it doubles to 2 G, and the structural and physiological cost rises quickly beyond that. The reason this connects back to stalls is the relationship every examiner wants to hear: stall speed increases with the square root of load factor. At 2 G, the square root of two is about 1.41, so your stall speed climbs by roughly 41 percent. The wing that stalls at 50 knots straight and level will stall near 70 knots in a hard 2 G turn. That is the accelerated stall, and it is why aggressive maneuvering close to the ground is unforgiving.

04The four left turning tendencies

Single engine trainers pull left, especially at high power and high angle of attack, which is exactly the takeoff and climb. Four effects combine to cause it, and a complete answer names all four:

  • Torque reaction. The engine spins the propeller one way, so by Newton's third law the airframe is pushed to rotate the other way, loading the left main on the ground and rolling the aircraft left.
  • Spiraling slipstream. The propeller flings air backward in a corkscrew. That spiral strikes the left side of the vertical stabilizer and pushes the tail right, which yaws the nose left.
  • P-factor. At a high angle of attack, the descending blade on the right side takes a bigger bite of air than the ascending blade on the left, so the right side of the propeller produces more thrust and yaws the nose left.
  • Gyroscopic precession. The spinning propeller behaves like a gyroscope, so a pitch change shows up as a yaw 90 degrees later in the direction of rotation. This one is most noticeable in tailwheel aircraft as the tail comes up.

The practical takeaway is the one your instructor repeats on every climb: right rudder. Knowing the four causes is what turns that habit into understanding.

05Ground effect, briefly

Within roughly one wingspan of the surface, the ground interferes with the wingtip vortices and the downwash behind the wing. That reduces induced drag and the wing feels temporarily more efficient. It matters in two moments. On takeoff, an aircraft can lift off in ground effect below a speed that will sustain the climb, then sink back if you pull it off too early. On landing, the same effect produces the float just before touchdown. Respect it on both ends and it stops being a surprise.

?Frequently asked questions

At what airspeed does a wing stall?

At no fixed airspeed. A wing stalls when it exceeds its critical angle of attack, which can happen at any speed or attitude. The book stall speed assumes one G, max gross weight, and unaccelerated flight.

Why does the aircraft pull left on takeoff?

Four tendencies acting together: torque, spiraling slipstream, P-factor, and gyroscopic precession. At high power and high angle of attack, P-factor and spiraling slipstream dominate, so you feed in right rudder.

How does a turn change my stall speed?

Stall speed rises with the square root of load factor. A 60 degree level turn is 2 G, which raises stall speed by about 41 percent.

What is ground effect?

Reduced induced drag within about one wingspan of the ground. It can let you lift off below a safe climb speed, and it causes the float on landing.

None of this requires the math behind it. It requires understanding the cause, so that when the aircraft does something you can explain why and stay ahead of it. That is exactly what the examiner is listening for.
