US Airspace Classes, Finally Explained

Six letters, one upside-down wedding cake, and a set of rules that decides who you talk to, what equipment you carry, and where you simply can't go. Here's the whole system in plain English.

GROUNDWORK AVIATION · UPDATED JUN 2026 · 10 MIN READ

Airspace is the topic students memorise hardest and understand least, which is exactly backwards, because the system is built on one simple idea: the busier and faster the traffic, the more protection everyone needs. Every rule in this article flows from that single principle. Controlled airspace exists where ATC provides separation services; the classes just set how much structure surrounds you.

The United States uses five controlled classes (A through E, there is no Class F in the US) plus uncontrolled Class G. Let's walk down the cake from the top.

AClass Alpha: the flight levels

From 18,000 feet MSL up to FL600, it's all Class A, everywhere over the continental US. Every aircraft is on an IFR clearance, every altimeter is set to 29.92, and VFR flight is not permitted 14 CFR 91.135. As a private pilot you'll spend your career underneath it, but the oral exam loves asking where it starts.

BClass Bravo: the big terminals

Class B surrounds the nation's busiest airports, think LAX, Atlanta, JFK, in that famous upside-down wedding-cake shape, typically up to 10,000 feet MSL. It's the most demanding airspace a VFR pilot can enter:

  • An explicit clearance is required. Not just radio contact, you must hear the words "cleared into the Class Bravo." If you haven't heard them, you stay out 14 CFR 91.131.
  • Equipment: a two-way radio, a Mode C transponder, and ADS-B Out, and the transponder/ADS-B requirement extends through the "Mode C veil," the 30-nautical-mile ring around the primary airport 14 CFR 91.215 · 91.225.
  • Pilot requirements: at least a private pilot certificate, or a student/recreational/sport pilot with the specific training and endorsement, and some Class B airports prohibit student solos entirely.

CClass Charlie: the mid-size terminals

Class C wraps airports busy enough for radar approach control but short of Bravo traffic, typically a 5 NM inner core from the surface to about 4,000 feet AGL, with a 10 NM shelf above. Entry requires establishing two-way radio communication, and here lives the most-tested distinction in all of airspace: if the controller responds with your callsign ("Skyhawk 12345, standby"), communication is established and you may enter. If they say "aircraft calling, standby", no callsign, you stay out. You'll also need a Mode C transponder and ADS-B Out in and above the Charlie.

DClass Delta: the towered fields

Class D surrounds smaller towered airports, usually a 4-ish NM radius up to about 2,500 feet AGL. Entry requires the same established two-way communication as Class C, but there's no transponder requirement specific to Delta. Watch for the dashed blue ring on the sectional, and check the ceiling figure in brackets. When the tower closes for the night, many Deltas revert to Class E or G, and the chart supplement tells you which.

EClass Echo: controlled, but quiet

Class E is everything controlled that isn't A, B, C, or D, and it covers most of the chart. It typically starts at 700 or 1,200 feet AGL (the fuzzy magenta and blue shading), at the surface around some non-towered instrument airports (dashed magenta), or at 14,500 MSL where nothing else applies. No radio calls, no clearance, no transponder below 10,000. For VFR pilots, Echo's significance is almost entirely about weather minimums, which step up because IFR traffic may be sharing the air with you. GClass Golf: uncontrolled

Whatever's left under the Echo floor is Class G: no ATC services, no entry requirements, see-and-avoid in its purest form. It's usually a thin layer, surface to 700 or 1,200 AGL, but in the mountain West it can run much deeper. Lower traffic protection means lower weather minimums, and examiners love asking what Golf requires by day below 1,200 AGL (one statute mile and clear of clouds, if you're rehearsing).

+Two traps the oral sets

Speed limits. 250 knots below 10,000 MSL everywhere; 200 knots at or below 2,500 AGL within 4 NM of a Class C or D airport, and 200 knots under a Bravo shelf or in a VFR corridor through it 14 CFR 91.117. Your trainer will never get there, but the regulation is fair game.

"Communication established" vs "clearance received." Charlie and Delta need the first. Bravo needs the second. Mixing these up is the single most common airspace error in checkride orals, and it's exactly the kind of distinction the examiner probes with a scenario, not a definition. ?Frequently asked questions

Do I need a clearance to enter Class B?

Yes, and it must be explicit. "Cleared into the Class Bravo" or you stay outside, even if the controller is talking to you.

What's the entry difference between Class C and D?

Both need established two-way communication (your callsign in the controller's reply). Class C adds Mode C and ADS-B Out; Class D doesn't.

Is there Class F in the US?

No. ICAO defines it and some countries use it, but US airspace runs A, B, C, D, E, G.

Can student pilots fly in Class B?

Only with a specific endorsement for that Bravo, and a list of the busiest ones prohibit student solo operations entirely.

Read the system top-down one more time and notice the pattern: every step down the cake trades protection for freedom. Once you see airspace as a single sliding scale instead of six unrelated rule-sets, the memorisation mostly takes care of itself.
