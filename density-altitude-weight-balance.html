Density Altitude and Weight & Balance, Without the Panic

The calculation heavy corner of the checkride that students dread. Density altitude, weight and balance, and takeoff numbers, broken into the few ideas that actually drive every answer.

GROUNDWORK AVIATION · UPDATED JUN 2026 · 10 MIN READ

Performance and weight and balance is where a lot of students lose confidence, because it looks like arithmetic and feels like a trap. It is neither, once you see what the numbers are really telling you. Two big ideas carry almost the whole topic: how dense the air is, and where the weight sits. Everything else is reading a chart carefully.

01Density altitude: the air your aircraft actually feels

Your aircraft does not care what the altimeter reads. It cares how many air molecules are passing over the wing and through the engine. Density altitude is the single number that captures that, and the clean definition to give an examiner is this: density altitude is pressure altitude corrected for nonstandard temperature. It is the altitude at which the air density matches what your aircraft is currently experiencing.

Three things thin the air and push density altitude up. Pilots remember them as high, hot, and humid. High field elevation means less air to begin with. High temperature spreads the molecules apart. High humidity replaces some of the air with lighter water vapor. When density altitude is high, three things suffer at once. The engine makes less power because it draws in less oxygen. The propeller produces less thrust because it has less air to bite. The wing needs a higher true airspeed to generate the same lift. The result is a longer takeoff roll, a shallower climb, and a higher groundspeed on approach. On a hot day at a high elevation strip, that combination has ended a lot of flights badly, which is precisely why the examiner cares whether you understand it.

You will not usually compute density altitude by hand on the checkride. You will be expected to know what raises it, what it does to performance, and to find your numbers in the POH charts. To do that you start from pressure altitude, which is what the altimeter shows when you set it to 29.92, then you apply temperature.

THE MENTAL MODEL

High, hot, and humid all mean thinner air, and thinner air means less power, less thrust, and less lift. If you can explain why each of those three suffers, you have the whole performance discussion in hand.

02Why weight and balance is two separate questions

The phrase hides two different concerns. Weight asks whether the aircraft is too heavy. Balance asks whether the load sits in the right place. An aircraft can be under gross weight and still dangerously out of balance, so both have to check out.

Excess weight is the simpler of the two. A heavier aircraft needs more runway, climbs more slowly, has a higher stall speed, and a reduced useful ceiling. Stay at or below the maximum gross weight in the POH and that side is satisfied.

Balance is about the center of gravity, the point the aircraft effectively balances around, and it has to fall inside a published forward and aft range. Where it sits within that range changes how the aircraft handles.

03Forward and aft CG, and which one bites

A forward CG is the safe direction to err, but it is not free. With weight ahead of the limits the aircraft is more stable, which sounds good, but the tail has to produce more downforce to hold the nose up. That raises the effective load on the wing and therefore the stall speed, increases control forces so the aircraft feels heavy in pitch, and lengthens the takeoff roll. In the extreme you can run out of elevator authority to flare for landing.

An aft CG is the dangerous direction. As the center of gravity moves rearward the aircraft becomes less stable, the control forces lighten so it is easy to overcontrol, and the stall speed actually drops. The serious problem is recovery. With an aft CG a stall can develop into a spin that is difficult, or in the worst case impossible, to recover from, because there is not enough authority to bring the nose back down. This is why the aft limit is the one examiners treat with the most respect, and why you should too.

04The actual calculation, demystified

The math is one idea applied repeatedly. Every item of load produces a moment, which is just its weight multiplied by its arm, the distance from a fixed reference point called the datum. Heavier items and items farther from the datum produce larger moments.

To find the center of gravity you do three things. Add up all the weights to get total weight. Add up all the moments to get total moment. Divide total moment by total weight, and the answer is the center of gravity, measured as a distance from the datum. Then you confirm that both the total weight and that CG fall within the envelope the POH publishes. That is the entire procedure. A few standard weights make it quick: aviation gasoline is about 6 pounds per gallon, and engine oil about 7.5 pounds per gallon.

One detail examiners like to test is what happens as you burn fuel. Because the fuel tanks sit at a particular arm, using fuel shifts the CG, and in some aircraft a flight that starts inside the envelope can finish outside it. Checking weight and balance for both takeoff and landing, not just takeoff, is the mark of a careful pilot.

?Frequently asked questions

What is density altitude?

Pressure altitude corrected for nonstandard temperature. It is the altitude your aircraft performs as if it were at. High, hot, and humid conditions raise it and degrade performance.

Which is more dangerous, a forward or aft CG?

Aft. It reduces stability, lightens controls, and can make a spin unrecoverable. A forward CG is the safer direction, though it raises stall speed and control forces.

How much does avgas weigh?

About 6 pounds per US gallon. Oil is about 7.5 pounds per gallon. These standard figures are used in the weight and balance calculation.

How do I calculate CG?

Weight times arm gives each moment. Total the weights, total the moments, then divide total moment by total weight. Confirm the result sits inside the published envelope.

Strip away the dread and the topic is two questions and one repeated calculation. Understand what thins the air and where the weight sits, read the charts with care, and check both ends of the flight. That is all the examiner is asking you to show.
